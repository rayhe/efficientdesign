The Last Digital Speedmaster: Omega's X-33 Leaves the Catalog

Marcus Thorne
November 6, 2026
0.07 seconds per day
Average daily deviation of the Marstimer's thermo-compensated Calibre 5622 at 23 degrees Celsius. Omega's most accurate space watch, and the last digital Speedmaster the public could buy.

On November 3, 2026, Fratello reported that Omega had quietly removed the Speedmaster X-33 Marstimer from its catalog. Skywalker, the third generation of the line, had already disappeared some time earlier. Omega now sells no digital Speedmaster at all. Only one digital watch remains anywhere in the brand's catalog, the Seamaster Regatta, built for the 37th America's Cup in 2024. For 28 years the X-33 was Omega's answer to a question almost nobody else in Swiss watchmaking was asking, namely what happens when you stop designing a watch for the wrist and start designing it for a spacecraft cabin, a Mars transit, and a temperature range that would kill an ordinary quartz movement in a week.

Nothing about this was announced: no press release marked the end of a line that began in 1998, no farewell edition, no final production number. Catalog removals work like that: one day a reference is listed, the next it is not, and the only people who notice are the ones who were watching. Fratello noticed.

A Project Code That Became a Watch

X-33 started as an Omega research and development project code. Development began in the mid-1990s with input from General Thomas Stafford, the Apollo-era astronaut who had flown Gemini VI, Gemini IX, Apollo 10, and Apollo-Soyuz, and who understood what a flight watch needed to do inside a spacecraft. Stafford had flown four missions and run the astronaut office, and he knew from experience that the mechanical Speedmaster, for all its history, could not run a mission timer, sound an alarm loud enough to wake a sleeping crew member, or track three time zones at once, which is why the X-33's function list reads less like a watch specification and more like a flight operations checklist. Omega launched the first Speedmaster Professional X-33 in March 1998 at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, and kept the project code as the model name.

What arrived was an ana-digi hybrid: analog hands for instant reading, a digital display for everything else. Calibre 1666, a thermo-compensated quartz movement, held accuracy between minus 0.3 and plus 0.5 seconds per day across a temperature range of minus 20 to plus 70 degrees Celsius. It carried a chronograph measuring to one hundredth of a second, an 80-decibel alarm loud enough to be heard through a helmet and a spacecraft's ambient noise, mission elapsed time, and a backlight triggered by a pusher. NASA flight-qualified it the same year, and a 2013 U.S. government document called the X-33 the watch of choice for American astronauts.

Flight qualification is the detail that explains the next 28 years. Once a watch passes NASA's testing, every change to the design risks invalidating the qualification. Omega learned this with the mechanical Moonwatch, which stayed in production for decades partly because replacing it meant requalifying its replacement. With the X-33, the same logic applied in digital form.

Four Generations, One Template

Every X-33 followed the same template from prototype to Marstimer: a grade 2 titanium case, analog hands over a digital display, and functions chosen by astronauts rather than marketers. What changed between generations was the movement, the bezel, and the mission the watch was built to serve.

Second generation arrived in 2001 as a refinement, not a redesign: brushed bezel and pushers, a lume pip on the bezel, the same Calibre 1666 inside. In the summer of 2006 Omega ended public sales of the Gen 2, and the watch vanished from stores, but production did not stop, and Omega kept building Gen 2 watches for NASA under agency-only reference numbers, a quiet arrangement that has now outlasted the public life of every successor.

Skywalker came in December 2014, the first X-33 developed under an ESA patent licence based on an invention by ESA astronaut Jean-François Clervoy. Its Calibre 5619 was a nine-jewel thermo-compensated quartz chronograph with a ceramic bezel carrying a chromium nitride scale, a matte finish across every surface to kill reflections inside a spacecraft, and a negative LCD display that put all digital information in neat horizontal rows. ESA tested and qualified it for manned spaceflight. NASA never flight-certified it, which mattered more than any specification on the data sheet.

Marstimer followed in September 2022, built at ESA's request for Mars missions. Same 45mm grade 2 titanium case, same 30 meters of water resistance, but a new Calibre 5622 with 111 components plus 44 more in its electric module, and a function list no other watch on sale could match, yet it lasted only four years in the catalog.

Why Thermo-Compensation Was the Whole Point

Quartz is accurate until temperature moves. A standard quartz crystal's oscillation frequency drifts as it gets hotter or colder, and a spacecraft swings between extremes that would make a normal quartz watch gain or lose seconds a day. Thermo-compensation answers this with a temperature sensor and a correction table: the movement measures its own temperature and adjusts the division of the crystal's oscillations to cancel the drift, which is a small piece of embedded metrology doing the job that a balance wheel, a hairspring, and a regulator do in a mechanical watch, except that it does it silently, with no moving parts, and across a temperature range no mechanical movement is expected to survive.

That is the entire engineering argument for the X-33 line, and it is worth stating plainly because the rest of the watch is ordinary by modern standards. Calibre 1666 held minus 0.3 to plus 0.5 seconds per day from minus 20 to plus 70 degrees Celsius. Calibre 5622 tightened that to an average deviation of 0.07 seconds per day at 23 degrees. Accuracy is the product. For comparison, a COSC-certified mechanical chronometer is allowed minus 4 to plus 6 seconds per day, and most quartz watches on sale today drift around 15 seconds a month with no compensation at all, which means the Marstimer's movement was roughly seventy times more accurate than a certified mechanical chronometer is allowed to be, and about seven times more accurate than the uncompensated quartz watch on most wrists, and nobody buying one would ever have known it from looking at the dial.

The same mission-first thinking shows in the details, starting with an 80-decibel alarm that needs acoustic apertures in the titanium caseback, which is why the X-33's caseback is drilled rather than solid. A two-year battery life and a user-selectable power-saving mode matter when the nearest watchmaker is 400 kilometers below you. A backlight triggered by a pusher matters when the cabin lights are off and the LCD is negative, meaning dark digits on a darker background. Every one of these choices trades elegance for function, and every one of them is defensible on its own terms.

None of it is glamorous, and that is the point, because a flight instrument earns its keep in conditions the wearer hopes never to experience, and the X-33's best engineering is the kind you never notice until the temperature drops twenty degrees and the time is still right.

Telling Time on a Planet With a Longer Day

Marstimer's headline function sounds like a gimmick until you do the arithmetic. A Martian day, a sol, lasts 24 hours, 39 minutes and 35.244 seconds. Mars runs about 39 minutes longer than Earth every day, which means any clock that counts Earth hours will be wrong on Mars within a day, and useless within a week.

Omega's answer was a second, parallel timebase: Martian Time Coordinated, or MTC, tracks the sol date and time at Mars's prime meridian, the Martian equivalent of Greenwich. Two Martian time zones, M1 and M2, cover rover locations the way Earth time zones cover cities. Phase elapsed time and alarms run in Mars time as well as Earth time, so a mission phase can be timed in sols from touchdown. Switching between Earth time and Mars time takes a three-second press of the crown.

Running two calendars in one movement means solving a problem no Earth watch faces: Mars has its own leap years. A Martian year lasts 668.599 sols, so the Marstimer counts leap years of 668 and 669 sols, and weeks of 95 and 96 weeks, to keep its calendar aligned with the Martian solar year, which is the same kind of slow, accumulating correction that Earth calendars handle with a leap day every four years, except that Mars demands it in units of 39 extra minutes a day compounded across nearly two Earth years, and getting it wrong would put every rover schedule, every landing window, and every surface operation 39 minutes further out of sync with every passing sol. On top of that sits the solar compass, a true solar time function for Earth and Mars that uses the sun's position to find true north on either planet. All of it runs under an ESA patent licence, application PCT/EP2020/061140.

Mars time is not a complication but a second calendar, a second clock, and a second navigation instrument sharing one case. No other production watch has ever attempted it, and now no production watch attempts it at all.

Why NASA Still Flies the Old One

Here is the strangest fact in the whole story. On April 1, 2026, the Artemis II crew launched toward the Moon, and all four astronauts, Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen, wore second-generation X-33s, not the Skywalker and not the Marstimer, but the 2001 watch with the 1998 movement, which the public has not been able to buy for twenty years.

Qualification inertia explains it, because NASA flight-qualified the X-33 in 1998, and the Gen 2 inherited that qualification with minimal changes. Newer did not mean flown. Skywalker was ESA-qualified, not NASA-qualified, and astronauts are conservative about unproven equipment in the same way test pilots are conservative about unproven airframes. A database maintained by space and watch enthusiasts, "Watches used in space exploration," contains 388 references to the Omega Speedmaster X-33, from prototypes flown in 1996 to X-33s worn in 2026. Nearly all of them are Gen 1 and Gen 2.

There is also a qualification line the X-33 never crossed. NASA's only EVA-qualified wristwatch remains the mechanical Speedmaster Professional. X-33s fly inside the spacecraft, never outside it. So the watch that went to the Moon in 2026 was the digital one, and the watch that will walk on the Moon in 2028 will almost certainly be the mechanical one: both Speedmasters, and neither the newest one.

Why the Public Said No

Four years for the Marstimer, eleven for the Skywalker, eight for the Gen 1 and Gen 2 combined in public sale. The market voted. The commercial record is unambiguous: people did not buy this watch in the numbers Omega needed, and the reasons are not hard to find.

Price came first, since the Skywalker listed at $5,900 while the Marstimer cost CHF 5,900 before tax, about $7,000 in the United States. Those prices bought functions that a Casio costing a fraction of the amount also offered, alarms and timers and world time, in a smaller case with a longer battery life. Horology enthusiasts said so at the time, and they were not wrong on the arithmetic. What the arithmetic missed was the thermo-compensation, the qualification testing, and the titanium case, none of which shows up on a spec sheet next to a price.

Readability came second, because the Skywalker and Marstimer used negative LCD displays, light digits on a dark background, which look technical in photographs and wash out in bright light. Astronauts asked for the look, and astronauts work in controlled cabin lighting, while everyone else works in sunlight.

Size and water resistance finished the argument: forty-five millimeters across, nearly 15 millimeters thick, and 30 meters of water resistance. That is a watch built for a spacecraft cabin, where water resistance does not matter and wrist presence does not either, because the watch is worn over a sleeve or on a forearm. On a wrist in a city, it is a large, expensive, hard-to-read instrument that cannot be trusted near a pool.

Collectors buy watches that flatter the wrist and hold a story. The X-33 flattered neither the wrist nor the story; it flattered the mission.

Catalog Removal Is Not Discontinuation

Precision matters here, because the reporting has been loose and Omega has not announced a discontinuation. What Fratello reported, and what a second outlet confirmed, is that the Marstimer and the Skywalker no longer appear in Omega's public catalog. Omega continues to produce X-33 watches on demand for NASA and ESA, often under different reference numbers. A watch absent from a public catalog is not the same thing as a watch no longer made.

Still, the direction is clear: three public generations in 28 years, each shorter-lived than the last, and now zero on sale. Fratello's hope for a future X-33 is a positive display for readability and connectivity for data logging, both sensible, both overdue. Add solar charging, which would end the two-year battery replacement cycle, and a smaller case, which would end the most common complaint about wearing one. Keep the 80-decibel alarm, keep the thermo-compensation, and keep the titanium.

Whether Omega builds that watch depends on whether it believes a fifth generation can sell where four could not. Until then, the digital Speedmaster exists in exactly two places: on astronauts' wrists, and on the secondary market. The line is over. The watch is not.
