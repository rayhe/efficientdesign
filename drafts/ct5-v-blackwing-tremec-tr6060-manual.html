Six Gears Between Extinction and Immortality: The Tremec TR6060 in the CT5-V Blackwing

By Marcus Thorne May 20, 2026
880 Nm Maximum input torque rating of the TR6060 in Ratio Set E, the configuration used in the CT5-V Blackwing

Half of them chose the stick. That number is almost certainly wrong in ways that matter, because Cadillac never published a precise take-rate breakdown for the CT5-V Blackwing. But multiple sources inside the program and at GM Authority have pegged manual uptake near 50 percent across the model's production run, a figure so far above industry norms for performance sedans that it demands explanation. Think about that. Half. BMW's M3 manual take rate hovers around 30 percent. Porsche reports similar numbers for the 911 GT3. For a $97,000 Cadillac sedan weighing 4,100 pounds, half is staggering.

What those buyers chose was a Tremec TR6060 six-speed manual rated for 880 Nm of input torque, fitted with triple-cone synchronizers on first and second gears, running a bespoke ratio set found in no other production vehicle, and connected to the driver through a shift linkage that Cadillac's transmission development lead Geoff Holbein spent seven years refining across three vehicle platforms. When Cadillac announced the F1 Collector Series in May 2026, limited to 26 units with 685 horsepower and zero automatic option, they were not making a marketing gesture. They were acknowledging that the transmission is what made the Blackwing matter.

From T-56 to TR6060: What Changed and Why

Tremec's original T-56 six-speed entered production in 1992. Built for the Dodge Viper and subsequently adopted by GM for the Corvette, Camaro, and Pontiac GTO, it served the American performance market for over a decade with torque ratings between 350 and 550 lb-ft depending on the application. By the mid-2000s, the T-56's limitations had become structural. Engine outputs were climbing past 500 horsepower, vehicle weights were rising with safety regulations, and customer tolerance for harsh shifting in a $60,000-plus car had collapsed entirely.

Tremec's response was not incremental. It was architectural. Ford Australia's FG Falcon program provided the original development platform, and the resulting TR6060 shared the T-56's basic layout while replacing nearly every internal component. Where the T-56 used double-cone synchronizers on the lower gears, the TR6060 introduced triple-cone synchronizers on first and second with double-cone units on third through sixth and reverse. Triple-cone synchros multiply the friction surface area available during a gear engagement event by stacking three concentric friction cones inside the synchronizer assembly. More friction surface means lower pedal effort for the same synchronization work, which translates directly to lighter, smoother shifts at high input torques.

Gear construction changed fundamentally. Where T-56 speed gears were single-piece forgings, the TR6060 uses two-piece gears with laser-welded clutch teeth rings. Separating the clutch engagement teeth from the gear body allows each to be optimized independently. Gear tooth profiles can be cut for load capacity and noise reduction. Clutch teeth can be machined for precise engagement geometry without compromising the gear's structural material properties. On second and third gears, Tremec introduced asymmetric clutch teeth, where the tooth profile differs between the engagement and disengagement faces. Brilliant solution. Asymmetric teeth reduce the effort required to enter the gear while increasing the mechanical lock that holds it there, addressing a common complaint about the T-56 under aggressive track driving: gear pop-out during high-load deceleration shifts.

Shift detent control migrated from the T-56's ball-and-spring mechanism to broached grooves on the mainshaft with a spring-loaded anti-friction roller. Combined with anti-friction ball struts and fine-pitch splines on all synchronizer hubs, the result was a transmission that could handle 650 lb-ft while requiring less shift effort than the T-56 managed at 450. Every surface that moved against another surface during a shift event received either a roller, a sintered bronze friction material, or a geometric redesign intended to reduce parasitic resistance. Shift feel as a design output. Not a side effect.

Ratio Set E: Tuned for a Sedan, Not a Sports Car

Tremec offers the TR6060 in six standard gear ratio configurations, labeled A through F, with custom ratios available on request. Each set targets a different vehicle archetype. Set A (3.01 first, 0.57 sixth) suits heavy vehicles needing deep first-gear multiplication and tall highway overdrive. Set C (2.66 first, 0.63 sixth) appeared in the Camaro ZL1 and CTS-V, prioritizing closer spacing in the middle gears for track work.

Cadillac selected Ratio Set E for the CT5-V Blackwing, specifying 2.29, 1.61, 1.21, 1.00, 0.82, and a sixth gear of 0.54 that does not appear in any of Tremec's published standard configurations. Nobody else runs it. That 0.54 overdrive is exceptionally deep. For context, the Corvette C6 ZR1 used the same Ratio Set E for gears one through five but paired it with a 0.68 sixth gear. Cadillac went 20 percent deeper in overdrive, accepting higher driveline speed at a given vehicle velocity in exchange for reduced engine rpm on the highway.

At 70 mph with the Blackwing's 2.73:1 rear axle ratio and 275/35R19 rear tires, the LT4 V8 turns roughly 1,450 rpm in sixth gear. That is below the engine's peak torque point by a wide margin, deep enough that the supercharger bypass valve is open and the blower is unloaded, spinning freely without compressing air. Basically invisible. Fuel consumption at cruise approaches that of the naturally aspirated LT1. It is a transmission ratio selected by engineers who understood that a sedan spends 90 percent of its life at part throttle, even if it can produce 668 horsepower at wide-open.

First gear at 2.29 is notably shorter than the Viper's (2.26) or Hellcat Challenger's (2.26), reflecting the Blackwing's greater curb weight. A heavier vehicle needs more mechanical advantage to launch cleanly from a stop without excessive clutch slip, particularly with 659 lb-ft of torque available off idle thanks to the supercharger's instant boost response. But 2.29 is considerably taller than the Camaro SS's 3.01 first gear, because the Blackwing's character is not about stoplight launches. It is about accessible torque across a wide operating band without constant shifting in traffic.

Synchronizer Engineering: Why Triple Cones on First and Second

A synchronizer's job is deceptively simple to describe and extraordinarily difficult to execute well. When the driver moves the shift lever, a fork pushes a sliding sleeve toward the target gear. Before the sleeve's internal splines can engage the gear's clutch teeth, the synchronizer's friction cones must equalize the rotational speed between the mainshaft (connected to the wheels through the driveshaft) and the target gear (spinning at a speed determined by the previous gear ratio and engine rpm). Until those speeds match, the sleeve cannot engage. Push harder, and you grind. Push correctly, and the synchronizer does its work in a fraction of a second.

In a single-cone synchronizer, one conical friction surface does all the speed-matching work. It works. Double-cone adds a second concentric surface, roughly doubling the available friction area. Triple-cone adds a third. More friction area means the same synchronization work happens at lower applied force, which means the driver's hand feels less resistance during the shift.

Tremec places triple-cone synchronizers on first and second in the TR6060 because those gears demand the most synchronization work. A 2-1 downshift at track speeds requires equalizing a large speed differential between mainshaft and target gear, and the synchronizer must absorb significant rotational energy to do so. On an engine producing 659 lb-ft through a dual-mass flywheel, the rotational inertia entering the synchronizer pack is substantial. Triple cones manage that energy without requiring the driver to pause mid-shift or apply excessive lever force, maintaining the illusion that the transmission is smaller and lighter than it is.

Tremec specifies hybrid and sintered bronze friction materials across the synchronizer pack. Sintered bronze offers high friction coefficient with excellent heat dissipation, critical during repeated hard shifts where traditional paper-based friction materials would glaze and lose effectiveness. Hybrid synchronizers combine bronze friction surfaces with steel structural components, optimizing the weight-to-friction-area ratio. Track conditions are merciless. At Laguna Seca, where a session might involve 200 downshifts in 20 minutes, material selection in the synchronizer pack is not academic. It determines whether shift feel degrades on lap 15 or remains consistent through lap 30.

Shift Linkage: Where Cadillac Spent the Time

Geoff Holbein joined Cadillac's manual transmission program on the ATS-V and carried it through the Camaro, Corvette, and both Blackwing models. His team was responsible for everything between the shift knob and the synchronizer sleeve: the shifter ratio, the linkage geometry, the vibration isolation, and the calibration of Active Rev Match. Where Tremec builds the gearbox, Cadillac builds the experience.

For the CT5-V Blackwing, Holbein's team selected a 5.1:1 shifter ratio, matching the ratio used in GM's 1LE track-package Camaros. Previous Cadillac V-series cars used a 5.6:1 ratio, producing longer throws with less mechanical advantage at the lever. Shorter throws feel sportier but transmit more of the transmission's internal resistance to the driver's palm, which creates a problem in a Cadillac: the brand's customer base expects refinement even in a 668-horsepower sedan. Holbein's solution was to reduce throw distance while simultaneously reducing friction everywhere in the shift path, so the shorter throw felt light rather than notchy. Precision, not force.

A new cam-and-plunger detent system replaced the previous generation's mechanism. Anti-friction rollers in the detent grooves reduced the force spikes that create the "click" sensation as the lever passes through the gate. In a Camaro, those clicks feel purposeful. In a Cadillac, they feel cheap. Two different problems. Holbein described the goal as eliminating all transmission-generated noise and vibration from the cabin, leaving only the engine and exhaust to provide the sensory feedback. Let the powertrain sing. Keep the gearbox silent.

Remote shifter flex had been a persistent complaint on GM's Alpha and Omega platform cars, where the shifter mounts to the body and connects to the transmission through a mechanical linkage rather than sitting directly on the gearbox. During hard cornering, chassis flex could deflect the linkage enough to cause binding or imprecise gate engagement. Texas Drivetrain, a well-known Tremec specialist, notes that eliminating the remote shifter entirely (replacing it with a solid-mounted unit directly on the transmission) cures the issue completely but requires a custom crossmember and driveshaft. For the Blackwing, Cadillac's engineers stiffened the linkage mounts and revised the bushing compliance rather than abandoning the remote design, preserving the lower NVH characteristics that a body-mounted shifter provides over a transmission-mounted one.

Active Rev Match: The Computer as Courtesy

Active Rev Match in the CT5-V Blackwing blips the throttle automatically during downshifts to match engine speed to the target gear's rotational velocity before the clutch re-engages. It is, in mechanical terms, heel-toe downshifting performed by the engine management system rather than the driver's right foot. Computers are faster. Always. Sensors on the clutch pedal, transmission input shaft, and vehicle speed module feed the ECU a continuous picture of drivetrain state. When the system detects clutch disengagement followed by a shift lever movement toward a lower gear, it commands a throttle opening calibrated to land engine rpm at the correct speed for the new gear ratio by the time the clutch pedal returns.

No-lift shift works in the opposite direction. During upshifts at wide-open throttle, the system cuts ignition timing and opens the throttle slightly during the shift event to maintain turbo (or in this case, supercharger) boost pressure across the ratio change. On the Blackwing's LT4, the Eaton R1740 TVS supercharger produces boost proportional to engine speed. A momentary rpm drop during an upshift causes a corresponding boost drop, creating a torque hole between gears. No-lift shift minimizes that hole by keeping the throttle plate open while the driver pulls through the gate, allowing the supercharger to maintain rotor speed and discharge pressure through the transition.

Both systems can be disabled. One button. Press the Rev Match button on the center console, and the blips stop. You are on your own with the pedals. Many owners report running this way permanently, viewing the electronic assistance as training wheels. Others leave it engaged in traffic and disable it on track, where the rhythm of manual rev-matching is part of the point. Cadillac's decision to make it defeatable rather than mandatory reflects an understanding of the buyer: the kind of person who chooses a manual transmission in a $97,000 sedan is the kind of person who has opinions about how their downshifts should sound.

50 Percent: What the Take Rate Reveals

Industry context makes the Blackwing's manual take rate remarkable. When Cadillac's performance variant manager Mirza Grebovic announced in December 2020 that the Blackwing would offer a manual transmission as standard equipment, the internal forecast projected 25 to 35 percent uptake. That estimate came from Cadillac chief engineer Tony Roma, who studied take rates across the competitive set and noted that most performance cars with manual options see single-digit to low-twenties adoption. For a luxury sedan, 35 percent would have been exceptional.

Actual uptake ran closer to 50 percent. Why? The conventional explanation is enthusiasm: Blackwing buyers are disproportionately driving enthusiasts who selected the car precisely because it offered a manual in a segment where alternatives had abandoned one. But there is an engineering explanation too. Holbein's team made the manual version faster to 60 mph than the automatic (3.4 seconds versus 3.7, per MotorTrend testing), which inverts the usual hierarchy where automatic transmissions are quicker off the line. A dual-mass flywheel and the LT4's massive low-rpm torque give the manual Blackwing a launch advantage that the 10-speed auto's torque converter cannot replicate under hard standing starts, because the clutch engagement is more direct and the driver controls the slip point rather than the software.

Resale data reinforces the pattern. Manual CT5-V Blackwings are selling on the secondary market above original MSRP, with clean examples regularly clearing $100,000. Automatic cars sell at or slightly below sticker. Collectors already treat the manual Blackwing as the definitive version, the one that will be remembered and wanted in 20 years. Whether those collectors are right about long-term value is speculation. But their money is not.

F1 Collector Series: 685 Horsepower, Manual Only

Cadillac unveiled the CT5-V Blackwing F1 Collector Series at the Miami Grand Prix in May 2026, limited to 26 units to mark the year the team joined the Formula 1 grid. Output rises to 685 horsepower and 673 lb-ft of torque courtesy of a modified Eaton R1740 TVS supercharger with a smaller drive pulley (raising the drive ratio from 3.14:1 to 3.24:1) and a taller CNC billet supercharger cover. We covered that supercharger work in detail previously. What matters here is the transmission decision.

Manual only. No automatic option for a $153,000-plus car limited to 26 units where offering both would have been trivially easy from a manufacturing standpoint, since both transmissions are already validated for the platform. Cadillac chose to exclude the automatic deliberately, and the decision makes sense only if you understand what the TR6060 represents in this car. It is not a powertrain option. It is the identity.

At 685 horsepower, the F1 Collector Series pushes the TR6060 to the edge of its published torque rating. Tremec rates Ratio Set E at 880 Nm (649 lb-ft). GM's published figure for the F1 Collector Series is 673 lb-ft at the flywheel. The transmission is operating at approximately 96 percent of its rated capacity in a peak, transient condition that occurs for fractions of a second during wide-open-throttle acceleration in the lower gears. Sustained loading is significantly lower because the torque multiplication effect of the lower gear ratios means the transmission sees the highest loads only during initial launch and first-to-second upshifts. For the remaining 99.9 percent of driving, the TR6060 is managing loads well within its design envelope.

Structural Details: Why It Survives

Aluminum alloy cases in manual transmissions are a known compromise. Steel is stiffer and more dimensionally stable under thermal loading, but aluminum saves weight and dissipates heat faster. Tremec uses die-cast aluminum alloy for the TR6060's three-piece housing: main case, extension housing, and integrated clutch housing. Integrating the clutch housing into the main case eliminates a bolted joint that in the T-56 could develop micro-deflection under high torque loads, slightly misaligning the input shaft and causing increased bearing wear.

Both the output shaft and countershaft ride in triple-supported bearing arrangements. Taper bearings and cylindrical roller bearings handle radial and thrust loads at the shaft ends, while needle bearings support all speed gears on their respective shafts. Needle bearings reduce the friction between free-spinning gears and the shaft they ride on, which directly affects how smoothly the transmission freewheels in neutral and how little resistance the driver feels during cold starts.

Lubrication is Dexron III automatic transmission fluid rather than conventional gear oil. ATF has lower viscosity than traditional 75W-90 gear oil, reducing internal drag and improving cold-weather shift quality. GM certifies the TR6060's lubricant fill as lifetime, requiring no scheduled changes. At 3.45 liters (7.29 pints) capacity, the relatively generous oil volume provides a thermal buffer during track sessions. Total wet weight lands at 66.3 kg (146 pounds), which is remarkably light for a transmission rated at 880 Nm. For comparison, the ZF 8HP automatic in an M5 weighs over 87 kg and the GM 10L90 in the automatic Blackwing weighs approximately 95 kg.

What Dies With It

Production of the 2026 Cadillac CT5-V Blackwing continues at GM's Lansing Grand River Assembly plant through late 2026. Cadillac has confirmed the CT5 nameplate will return in a next-generation form, likely as a 2028 model, with internal combustion power. Whether that future CT5 will offer a manual transmission, a V8 engine, or both remains unconfirmed. What is confirmed is that no other American manufacturer currently plans to build a manual V8 sedan. Dodge retired the Challenger and Charger. Chevrolet's Camaro is gone. Ford never offered a manual V8 sedan in the first place.

Tremec, meanwhile, has moved its engineering focus toward dual-clutch transmissions. The TR-9080 DCT in the C8 Corvette and the TR-9070 DCT in the Ford Mustang GTD represent the company's current OEM direction, applying decades of gear and synchronizer expertise to automated shifting mechanisms where a computer does the work that a human hand used to do. The TR6060 continues in production for the Blackwing and through the aftermarket Magnum line, where its DNA lives on in a $2,800 crate unit that hot-rodders bolt into LS-swapped muscle cars. But as an OEM component in a new vehicle from a major manufacturer, the TR6060's days are numbered in months.

This is the part where automotive journalists usually lament the death of the manual transmission in mournful, predictable terms. I will skip that. Automatics and dual-clutch transmissions are faster, more efficient, and more consistent than any human operator working a clutch pedal and a shift lever. The TR6060 in the Blackwing survives not because it is better, but because it offers something different: the physical act of choosing a gear, feeling the synchros engage, modulating clutch slip against throttle input, building a rhythm between machine and driver that no software can replicate because the entire point is that you, specifically, are the one doing it.

Tremec built the hardware. Holbein and his team at Cadillac tuned the experience. Fifty percent of Blackwing buyers voted with their purchase orders, and when Cadillac built the most powerful version of the car ever produced, they made the manual the only option. Six gears, triple-cone synchros, a 3D-printed medallion on the shift knob, and a ratio set that does not exist anywhere else in the Tremec catalog. It weighs 146 pounds and handles 880 newton-meters and it is going away. But the 26 owners of an F1 Collector Series, and the thousands who chose a manual Blackwing before them, will not care what replaces it. They chose the stick.
