Never Set It Again: How Fears Squeezed a Perpetual Calendar Into 9.4 Millimeters

A perpetual calendar is a mechanical computer that knows February has 29 days in a leap year, and the hard part was never the calendar; it was the thickness. Fears spent four years on the answer, then did the strangest thing in the segment: they did not limit it.

By Elena Voss · October 15, 2026 · Watches

A computer that fits in a case

Watch people call the perpetual calendar a complication. It is more honest to call it a mechanism with opinions about the Gregorian calendar.

Inside the movement sits a program wheel, a disc with forty-eight teeth, one for every month of the four-year leap cycle, and its teeth are cut in two depths. Deep teeth stand for 31-day months, shallow teeth for the short ones, and one tooth carries a deeper notch still for the February of a leap year. As months pass, feeler levers read the tooth depths and decide how many steps the date wheel advances at month end. That is the whole trick: a four-year memory, encoded in brass, read by levers.

Set it correctly once and it tracks day, date, month, and leap year with no further attention until the year 2100, when the Gregorian rule's exception to the exception, the century that is not divisible by 400, breaks every perpetual calendar ever made. One hundred years of self-sufficiency is the deal, and watchmakers take it gladly.

Mechanically, though, perpetual calendars have a packaging problem. The module sits on top of a base movement, and height stacks: base plus module plus dial plus hands plus crystal. Thin base movements get a perpetual calendar bolted on, and the result is often a case that wears like a hockey puck. Jaeger-LeCoultre's Master Ultra Thin Perpetual manages 9.2 millimeters, and it manages it with a fully integrated manufacture caliber. Fears' answer to the same problem is 9.4 millimeters, and it manages it another way entirely.

The module decision

Strip the caseback off the Brunswick 39 Perpetual Calendar and the winding system tells the story before anything else. A solid 18-karat gold rotor, engraved with the Bristol flower and the Fears cypher, turns on a Dubois Dépraz 55115, a perpetual calendar module mounted on a Sellita SW300 automatic base, finished with frosting and hand-polished anglage in dark rhodium.

Choosing a module over an in-house caliber is a decision some collectors treat as a confession. It should not be. Dubois Dépraz has been building calendar modules since the 1970s, and the 55115 is what the company calls its slimmest perpetual module architecture; it is the same logic that puts a Bosch inverter in a boutique EV. You buy the part from the specialist so your four years of development go into the watch instead of the wheel geometry.

What Fears did buy with its development time shows in the base. The SW300, the thin automatic in Sellita's catalog, is a 3.6-millimeter three-hander that beats at 28,800 vibrations per hour. Starting thin matters, because every millimeter in the movement stack is a millimeter in the case, and a perpetual calendar module cannot be made from nothing. Per the launch materials, the brand's four-year program went largely into case architecture around that stack, and the result is the slimmest Brunswick the company has made: 9.4 millimeters of case, plus a 0.7-millimeter domed sapphire crystal.

There is a second discipline in the module choice, and it is about service. A module that the industry knows, on a base that every watchmaker has seen, means this watch can be serviced in twenty years by someone other than the brand. Try that with a one-off manufacture caliber from a company that might not exist in 2100. The gold rotor says ambition; the SW300 underneath says the watch is meant to outlive the marketing.

A cushion from 1924

Perpetual calendars usually dress in round gold cases, as if the complication had a dress code. Fears instead reached into its own archive and pulled out a gentleman's cushion case from 1924, then re-cut it for the module.

That heritage gesture lands on genuinely new geometry. Bezel and caseback carry a concave profile, a first for the Brunswick line, and the concave treatment does visual work: it breaks the case's side into brushed and polished planes, with wide polished bevels catching light against flat brushed surfaces, and it reads as thinner than it measures. Fears fits its bespoke onion-shaped crown, signed with the cypher, and four discreet corrector pushers sit in the case band, one per calendar function.

Correctors deserve a word, because they are the user interface of every perpetual calendar and most brands hide them. Small recessed pushers, one each for day, date, month, and moonphase, require a stylus and a steady hand. Once set, per Fears, the calendar needs nothing again until 2100. Those pushers exist for the initial setup and for the one day in four years when you forget to wind it past the date change. Fears ships each watch with its corrector tool, which is the right call; a QP adjusted with a paperclip is a crime scene.

Water resistance is 50 meters, which is not nothing. A QP with four case-band pushers sealed to 5 ATM means real gasketing around apertures most makers would excuse as ornamental. It will not dive, but it survives a splash and a rainstorm, and for a complication usually treated as a vault piece, that is a statement about how the brand expects this watch to be worn.

The moon is finished with soot

Turn the watch over to the dial side and the materials get interesting in a quieter way. Frosted and coated in pure silver for a warm, bright surface the brand calls Silver Mink, the dial is made in Germany by a dial specialist, because frosted silver dials are a supplier art.

Then there is the moonphase. The moon disc is black lacquer, and the two silver moons are finished by hand using Charbonage, the technique where the watchmaker works candle soot into the surface to build a textured, luminous silver. It is one of the oldest finishing methods in the book, older than electric light, and it survives because nothing else gives a moon that particular hand-made grain. Under the moon, the subdials carry circular grooves with black and cyan-blue printing, and the leap-year indicator is tonal, set to disappear into the dial rather than shout.

Hands are the Fears pipette shape, tri-beveled and diamond-polished, plated in black gold. Applied indices get the same black gold treatment, quad-beveled. Against the frosted silver, the black gold reads almost black in flat light and flashes warm at an angle, which keeps the dial legible without clutter. Nothing here glows. A QP does not need to glow.

The strangest decision in the segment

Here is the part of the story that does not belong in any engineering column, and it is the part that makes the watch matter.

No velvet rope attaches. It joins the core collection, individually and sequentially numbered, priced at £26,950 including VAT, $28,500 in the US, CHF 26,484.50 in Switzerland.

This is nearly unprecedented at the complication level. Perpetual calendars arrive in numbered runs of fifty or a hundred, priced as collector events, because the economics of small-batch movement work demand it and because scarcity is easier to sell than engineering. Fears has decided the opposite: that its first high complication should be a watch you can actually buy, repeatedly, for as long as the brand cares to make it.

There is a business argument underneath the romance. Fears was founded in Bristol in 1846, closed in the quartz crisis, and was revived by the founder's great-great-great-grandson, Nicholas Bowman-Scargill. On the office wall, per the brand's telling, hang portraits of the four managing directors, plus a fifth frame kept deliberately empty, for the successor. A company telling that story cannot sell its first perpetual calendar as a fifty-piece event and call it heritage; heritage is the thing you keep making. Bowman-Scargill has said the brand's peak output was in the hundreds of thousands, and that it can work back toward scale. The perpetual calendar as a standing product is the mechanical version of that ambition.

Whether the market rewards it is an open question. Twenty-eight and a half thousand dollars is not pocket change, and the British watch revival still has more promise than proof. But the logic is sound: a perpetual calendar is a machine for never being set again, and a machine deserves a production line, not a velvet rope.

What to watch for

One honesty note belongs here, because the module architecture sets the terms of a real limitation. The Dubois Dépraz 55115, like most modular perpetuals, corrects the date forward only. Advance past the date-change window, which on most modules means roughly 9 PM to 3 AM, while the mechanism is mid-step, and you can jam the calendar works. Module makers and brands both print the warning, and owners of every brand's perpetual calendar learn the same lesson the same way. Fears' literature does not over-claim here, and the safe behavior is the standard one: never adjust a perpetual calendar near midnight, and if it stops, send it to someone who has set one before.

Fears ships each watch with a corrector tool and, for buyers who want it, a SwissKubik winder in Fears Blue aluminum with Bluetooth programming, which is the modern answer to the oldest QP maintenance problem: the calendar only stays correct if the watch stays wound. A winder is not an accessory for this watch. It is part of the mechanism's operating envelope.

Deliveries began in mid-September, and Fears is showing the watch at WatchTime New York this week, October 16 through 18 at Gotham Hall, alongside the industry's other independents. It is the right room: a British brand's first high complication, thin, moonlit, and, against all segment tradition, simply available. Watch people conflate complications with exclusivity constantly. This one declines the premise.
