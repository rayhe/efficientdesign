A Thermometer for Its Own Oil: Ressence TYPE 10 DN and the Second In-House Movement

Fourteen years of borrowed calibers ended in April with the TYPE 11. Seven months later, Ressence's second proprietary movement takes on the harder job: driving a display that swims in oil, through a wall it never touches.

By Marcus Thorne · November 4, 2026 · Watches

Ressence founder Benoît Mintiens has spent his career defending one stubborn idea: a watch dial should be a flat, legible surface, not a landscape of hands casting shadows across each other. His Ressence Orbital Convex System, ROCS, replaces hands with rotating discs that carry the time around a convex dial. Fill the space above those discs with oil and something remarkable happens to the optics. Oil and sapphire have similar refractive indices, so the gaps between discs visually disappear and the dial appears printed directly onto the underside of the crystal, with no reflections off the dial surface and no parallax. It looks less like a watch and more like a photograph of time.

Beautiful, and also mechanically hostile. Every disc in a ROCS module drags through a viscous fluid, and viscous drag eats torque the way a headwind eats fuel. For fourteen years Ressence fed that drag with other companies' movements, modified ETA bases tuned to deliver enough torque for the job. It worked, and the compromise showed in the specification sheet: 36 hours of power reserve, where a conventional watch with the same base caliber would run far longer, since oil takes its cut.

TYPE 11, unveiled in April 2026, was the first answer to that problem and it sidestepped the oil entirely. Its RW-01 movement drove a display that was not oil-filled, which let Ressence prove it could design and build a proprietary caliber with Concepto, the Swiss movement specialist, without fighting fluid dynamics at the same time. TYPE 10 DN, unveiled October 16 at WatchTime New York 2026 and shipping this November, is the second act. It puts the proprietary movement back into the oil. That is a much harder brief, and the specification sheet shows the work: 60 hours of reserve, up from 36, from a twin-barrel architecture built specifically for the hydraulic load.

Two chambers and a wall 0.1 mm thick

Inside the 41.5 mm Grade 5 titanium case sit two sealed chambers that never meet. Below, the RW-04 automatic caliber runs in air: 252 components, two barrels, 28,800 vibrations per hour. Above, the ROCS 10 module runs in oil: 196 components immersed in 2.95 ml of silicone-based fluid, carrying hours, minutes, a second time zone, and a 120-second runner on three eccentric biaxial satellites inclined at 9.75° and 17°. Between them, a titanium membrane 0.1 mm thick. No shaft passes through it and nothing penetrates it at all.

Motion crosses the wall magnetically, as a disc fitted with micro-magnets turns on the air side, driven by the movement, facing a matching disc inside the oil. Magnetic attraction carries the rotation across the membrane, with the two magnet sets sitting just 0.2 mm apart. Nine micro-magnets do the coupling, according to Ressence's launch materials. Think of it as a drivetrain with no driveshaft, which is exactly the point. Any physical penetration of the oil chamber would need a dynamic seal, and a dynamic seal in a fluid environment is a leak waiting for a date. Ressence chose to pay the efficiency cost of magnetic coupling rather than accept a seal that could fail. Fourteen years of oil-filled production suggest the bet was sound. What changed is that the movement on the air side is now theirs, shaped from the start for the load on the other side of the wall.

Torque is the throughline of the RW-04's design. Serial-coupled barrels, two of them, flatten the torque curve across the full 60-hour reserve instead of delivering a strong start and a fading finish. That matters more here than in a conventional watch because the load is not just friction in the gear train. It is the continuous drag of 196 components moving through silicone oil, plus the magnetic coupling itself, which loses some fraction of its transmitted energy to the air gap. Ressence does not publish a coupling efficiency figure, and that omission is worth noting rather than glossing over. A movement with only 60 hours of reserve driving a viscous load through a magnetic gap lives or dies on how much mainspring energy actually reaches the display, and the number that would settle the question is not public.

Oil expands, so Ressence put a gauge on it

Here is the part of the TYPE 10 DN I admire most, and it has nothing to do with the movement. Silicone oil expands and contracts with temperature, measurably, and a sealed chamber full of it has to go somewhere when it grows. Ressence's answer is an expansion membrane, a bellows-like chamber that absorbs the volume change and prevents gas bubbles from forming as the oil cools. Standard practice for the brand, carried over from earlier oil-filled types. What is new is the admission on the dial.

Inside the hour subdial sits a color-coded indicator that reports the oil's temperature. Green marks the normal operating range, roughly 25°C plus or minus 10°. Drift toward either extreme of the scale and the watch is telling you, in plain color, that its hydraulic system is approaching its limits. Every oil-filled Ressence is temperature tested before shipping, one hour at 60°C and one hour at minus 5°C, which brackets the green zone with a wide margin. Still, the gauge is a remarkable piece of honesty in an industry that usually hides its physics. Most mechanical watches are temperature sensitive too. Balance springs drift with heat, lubricants thicken in cold, and no conventional dial admits any of it. Ressence instruments the vulnerability and puts it where the owner can see it, which is either confidence or provocation depending on your tolerance for being told your watch has weather.

There is a second, quieter job hidden in the display. That 120-second runner, the fast-spinning subdial that gives the dial its sense of motion, doubles as a shock absorber for the magnetic transmission. A wrist in motion delivers impulses to the case, and impulses disturb a magnetic coupling more than they disturb a gear train, since there is no mechanical mesh holding the two sides in phase. A spinning mass resists sudden changes to its rotation. By making the runner do double duty as a flywheel damper, Ressence steadies the coupling through exactly the shocks that would otherwise risk a momentary slip between the magnet discs. It is the kind of dual-purpose solution that appears when a design constraint is severe enough to forbid single-purpose parts.

What the case is doing

Grade 5 titanium is the right material here for reasons beyond the usual weight argument, though the weight argument is strong: 82 grams for a 41.5 mm watch with 538 total components is genuinely light, and the dark blue DLC coating applied with Ressence's HarDeCo process gives the Dark Night edition its name. Titanium matters structurally too. That 0.1 mm membrane between the chambers is titanium, chosen because it must be thin enough for magnets to reach across it, stiff enough not to flex under pressure differentials, and inert enough to live against silicone oil for years. A membrane that corrodes, creeps, or work-hardens is a failed watch, and the material choice is doing quiet work that no owner will ever see.

Elsewhere the case is pure Ressence house style, refined rather than reinvented. A fixed bezel built from 24 individual titanium segments alternates lume-filled sandblasted inserts with brushed sections. Water resistance is 50 meters through the Ressence Compression Lock System. There is no crown, because a crown stem would have to penetrate a case whose entire architecture exists to avoid penetrations, so winding and time setting happen through the caseback. Owners of earlier oil-filled Ressence watches will recognize all of this. Newcomers should understand the trade being made: caseback setting is less convenient than a crown, and Ressence accepted that inconvenience to keep the seal architecture simple, choosing discipline over convenience.

What it does not do

Transparency first: I have not handled a TYPE 10 DN, and this analysis rests on published specifications, launch coverage, and the physics of the systems described. Hands-on time would answer questions that paper cannot, starting with legibility. Orbital displays demand a learning curve. Reading the time means locating the correct satellite, tracking it against fixed indices, and composing the reading from moving references. Owners adapt, according to Ressence, but adaptation is not instant, and a watch that takes a week to read fluently is a watch with a real usability cost.

Service is the second open question. Ressence recommends service every three to five years for its oil-filled watches, comparable to conventional intervals, but the procedure is not comparable. Draining, flushing, refilling, and resealing an oil chamber sits on top of the standard movement overhaul, and only Ressence or its authorized service network can do it properly. Buyers are not just buying a watch. They are buying a service relationship with a small independent brand, for the life of the watch, and that relationship is part of the price whether or not it appears on the invoice.

Price deserves a direct look. CHF 45,000 excluding taxes is nearly double the TYPE 11's CHF 23,000, for a watch whose movement is arguably an evolution of the TYPE 11's RW-01 rather than a clean-sheet design. Some of that premium is the oil system itself: hand-filling and individually temperature-testing sealed chambers does not scale the way assembling a conventional movement does. Some of it is the 100-piece limitation. Still, the gap invites the question of whether the oil display is worth roughly CHF 22,000 over the same brand's proprietary-movement watch without it. My answer, having never worn either, is that the oil display is the entire reason Ressence exists, and judging it as an option on top of a movement misses the point. Without the oil, Ressence is a clever display company. With it, Ressence is the only company doing this at all. That uniqueness is what the premium buys, and uniqueness is a defensible thing to charge for, even when it is not a comfortable thing to pay.

Why the second movement matters more than the first

First proprietary movements are statements. Second ones are evidence. RW-01 proved Ressence could design a caliber. RW-04 proves the company can aim its movement capability at its hardest problem and improve the numbers that mattered: reserve up from 36 hours to 60, torque delivery shaped for a viscous load, and a display that now reports on the health of its own hydraulic system. Vertical integration is often framed as a prestige play in watchmaking, a way to print "manufacture" on the dial. Here it reads as something more practical. Ressence spent fourteen years learning exactly what its display demands from a movement, and the RW-04 is what that education looks like when it is finally allowed to design the answer instead of adapting someone else's.

That temperature gauge stays with me. Watchmaking has a long tradition of hiding its compromises behind beautiful surfaces, and the TYPE 10 DN breaks that tradition in the most Ressence way possible: by turning a compromise into a feature and putting it on the dial in color. Oil expands. Magnets slip. Heat matters. Most watches pretend otherwise. This one tells you the truth, in green, and trusts you to find that reassuring rather than alarming. After fourteen years of borrowed movements and a first proprietary caliber that dodged the oil entirely, Ressence has built the movement its idea always needed, and then added an instrument to watch it work. That is not a flex but a company that finally trusts its own engineering enough to show you the gauges.
