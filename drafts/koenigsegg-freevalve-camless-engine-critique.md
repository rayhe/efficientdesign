## Critics Panel (7 critics)

### 1. Technical Accuracy (9.2/10)
The EHPA mechanism description is precise — solenoid trigger, hydraulic pilot valve, pneumatic spring return. Correct attribution of pneumatic valve springs to Renault's 1986 F1 program (Lotus 98T / Senna). TFG specs verified against multiple sources (600 hp, 2.0L, 70 kg, 95mm bore, 93.5mm stroke). The Qoros Qamfree numbers (230 hp from 156 hp base, 47% increase) check out. Minor: the claim that Senna's engine produced "around 1,200 horsepower" is the commonly cited peak qualifying figure but was never officially confirmed by Renault — a hedge like "reportedly" covers this appropriately and is present. SAE paper references are specific (2022-01-1066) and verifiable.

### 2. Engineering Depth (9.4/10)
Exceptional layering. The article moves from the fundamental mechanical constraint (fixed cam lobe profile) through the actuator mechanism, into system-level consequences (throttle body elimination, internal EGR, sequential turbo via valve control, combustion cycle switching), and then to production economics. The two-stroke mode explanation is particularly well-handled — noting the ~4,500 RPM practical limit and 80% volumetric efficiency rather than breathlessly celebrating the capability. The comparison to Honda VTEC, BMW Valvetronic, and Toyota Atkinson as partial approximations of what Freevalve does completely is the right framing for an engineering audience.

### 3. Readability & Structure (8.8/10)
Clean section hierarchy. The opening two paragraphs set up the camshaft as solved-problem-that-is-also-a-compromise, which is a strong hook for engineering-minded readers. "What Eliminating the Cam Actually Changes" section uses a numbered-consequence structure (First, Second, Third, Fourth) that works well for enumerable architectural differences. Some paragraphs in the combustion cycle switching section run long. The Gemera V8 pivot section handles an awkward narrative turn (the technology's flagship dropped it) with appropriate directness.

### 4. Prose Quality (9.0/10)
Sentence variety is excellent — short declaratives ("It works. It is cheap to manufacture...") alternate with longer technical explanations. No hedging language or unnecessary qualifiers. The closing line ("Koenigsegg built the proof. Someone still has to build the factory.") lands well. Avoids enthusiasm-inflation: phrases like "the engineering case remains strong" rather than "revolutionary breakthrough." Occasional passive voice in technical descriptions is appropriate to the subject matter.

### 5. Source Quality (8.6/10)
Eight sources cited, mixing primary (SAE papers, Koenigsegg interviews via CarBuzz/Goodwood) with reference material (Wikipedia for historical context). The SAE 2022-01-1066 paper and 2023 lifecycle analysis are strong primary sources. Would benefit from one more OEM-side source — e.g., published Freevalve AB specifications or Koenigsegg press materials directly. The Qoros section relies on contemporaneous auto press coverage, which is adequate for a defunct vehicle program. Renault F1 pneumatic valve spring history is well-established in motorsport literature.

### 6. Originality & Insight (8.9/10)
The "partial, mechanical approximation" framing — positioning VTEC, VANOS, Valvetronic, and Atkinson as incremental steps toward what Freevalve achieves completely — is a strong analytical contribution. Most Freevalve coverage focuses on horsepower numbers; this article spends more time on structural consequences (no throttle body, no external EGR, shorter cylinder head). The honest treatment of cost barriers and the Gemera V8 pivot avoids hagiography. The F1 pneumatic valve spring lineage adds genuine historical depth beyond the usual Koenigsegg-centered narrative.
