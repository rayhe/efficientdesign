### Critic 1: Technical Accuracy (9.5/10)
The altitude physics reasoning is exemplary. The 3% per 1,000 ft rule from Garrett Motion data, the air density calculation (58% of sea level at 14,115 ft), the pressure ratio math for turbochargers (2.2:1 at sea level vs 3.1:1 at summit) — all check out against engineering fundamentals and ISA standard atmosphere tables. The turbo recovery estimate (82-88% of sea-level power) is a defensible range consistent with published turbo engineering literature. The electric motor altitude-invariance claim is physically correct (electromagnetic torque generation is air-independent). ZTK downforce of 1,200 lb at 160 mph matches GM's published figures. The 42% reduction at altitude is mathematically consistent with 58% air density. Minor note: article states Shute's SendyCar V1 "set the rear-wheel-drive record" — the car is confirmed RWD per PPIHC.org, and its 2nd-overall finish at 8:29.497 almost certainly makes it the fastest RWD car in Pikes Peak history, but no source explicitly labels it a "RWD record." Technically defensible but not sourced.

### Critic 3: Originality (9.0/10)
Most coverage of this event is news reporting: "ZR1X set the record, here are the specs." This article asks and answers a genuinely different question: why is this particular powertrain architecture structurally advantaged at altitude? The pressure-ratio analysis for turbochargers at altitude is not something automotive journalism normally delivers. The battery cycling analysis (156 corners, ~3.6 seconds between turning events) is novel. The cooling paradox (colder but thinner air) is well-identified. The Hartford comparison as a natural experiment isolating the eAWD variable is clever framing. This reads like a technical brief that happens to be engaging prose.
